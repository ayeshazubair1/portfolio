    Retail Shrinkage & Inventory Optimization Analysis — Bibitor LLC

    Tools PostgreSQL, Tableau
    Duration Feb 2026 - Apr 2026

    Business Context

    Bibitor LLC operates ~80 wine and spirits stores across the state of Lincoln, generating $420–450M in annual sales with $300–350M COGS. Despite strong revenue, the company faces rising shrinkage and inventory imbalance across stores, with some locations overstocked while others frequently run out of high-demand products.

    The Challenge: Identify where inventory is disappearing, what's tying up capital, and the operational issues behind both.

    Executive Summary

    Bibitor LLC faces two major operational challenges: inventory shrinkage and excess inventory.

    This analysis identified $199.6K in positive revenue-loss estimates across 41 stores, alongside $28M in working capital tied up in slow-moving inventory across 8,198 SKUs.

    Shrinkage is highly concentrated, with four stores accounting for 65% of the positive revenue-loss estimates. Meanwhile, inventory imbalances and vendor-associated quantity variances highlight additional areas requiring attention across the retail network.

    These findings point to opportunities to strengthen inventory controls, improve stock allocation, reduce excess inventory, and introduce more robust receiving and reconciliation processes.

    Key Findings

    1. Shrinkage Is Highly Concentrated: Four Stores Drive Nearly Two-Thirds of Total Loss

    • Four stores (77, 25, 63, and 47) account for 65% of positive revenue-loss estimates, totaling $129.4K. This comprises $94.6K in estimated cost loss and $34.8K in estimated gross margin loss.
    • Store 77 represents the highest exposure, with $53K in estimated loss, potentially influenced by its larger SKU assortment.
    • This concentration suggests that prioritizing a small number of high-risk stores could deliver the greatest impact on inventory control.

    2. Operational Leakage Dominates: Low-Value Items Drive 88% of Shrinkage Value

    • Low-value items account for 88% of total shrinkage value ($234K of $264K), driven by transaction volume rather than unit price.
    • High-value items show higher per-unit loss rates but contribute less to total shrinkage.
    • This highlights the importance of strengthening controls around high-volume, low-value products, where small discrepancies can accumulate into significant financial exposure.

    3. Capital Inefficiency Represents the Largest Structural Risk: $28M Locked in Slow-Moving Inventory

    • Across the network, 8,198 SKUs exceed 90 days of supply, tying up $28M in working capital. Inventory is highly concentrated: four stores (77, 26, 49, and 25) account for $5.5M, with Store 77 alone contributing nearly $2M.
    • Stores 64, 77, 40, and 49 each hold more than 700 days of inventory, indicating substantial excess stock relative to sales velocity.
    • Reducing slow-moving inventory could improve cash flow, free working capital, and lower the risk of inventory becoming obsolete.

    4. Vendor Delivery Inaccuracies Create Systemic Stock Imbalances

    • Inventory variance is concentrated among specific vendors rather than distributed randomly.
    • William Grant & Sons Inc showed a cumulative negative variance exceeding 35K units, while Alisa Carr Beverages showed a positive variance of approximately 65% relative to recorded purchases.
    • Addressing a small number of high-variance vendors could significantly improve inventory accuracy across the network.

    5. Geographic Patterns Reveal Distinct Operational Failure Modes

    • Store-level inventory calculations identified locations with positive and negative inventory variances.
    • Stores 77, 25, and 63 recorded the highest positive estimated discrepancies, valued at $39K, $25K, and $16K, respectively. In contrast, Stores 49, 26, and 40 showed the largest negative estimated variances, valued at approximately $6.2K, $4.5K, and $3.4K.
    • These contrasting patterns suggest that inventory control and reconciliation priorities may differ across locations, supporting a more targeted approach to operational improvements.

    Recommendations

    1. Stabilize Inventory in High-Risk Stores

    • Critical stores (77, 25, 26, 49): Address overlapping shrinkage and excess-inventory signals through full inventory reconciliation, tighter receiving controls, frequent cycle counts, and temporary replenishment restrictions for SKUs exceeding 90 days of supply.
    • Emerging-risk stores (63, 47, 50, 45): Apply the same control framework at a lower frequency and monitor inventory trends for worsening performance.

    2. Reduce Leakage in Low-Value Items

    Target: High-volume, low-value SKUs.

    • Strengthen receiving verification and cycle counting for discrepancy-prone products.
    • Standardize replenishment and shelf-handling processes.
    • Reduce breakage and misplacement through tighter operational controls.

    3. Improve Vendor Delivery Accuracy

    Target: Vendors identified through the original quantity variance analysis.

    • Track ordered, received, and billed quantities to improve reconciliation accuracy.
    • Introduce variance thresholds to flag unusual quantity differences.
    • Conduct periodic vendor performance reviews using verified delivery records.

    Limitations & Assumptions

    1. Synthetic Dataset: Data is fictional; operational context such as store layout, staffing, security, and cycle counts is unavailable for deeper root-cause analysis.
    2. Temporal Granularity: Data is limited to annual snapshots (Start-of-Year and End-of-Year), preventing analysis of seasonal or monthly trends.
    3. Demand Assumption: Days of Supply assumes constant sales velocity and does not account for seasonality or spikes.
    4. Thresholding Approach: Risk thresholds (e.g., 90-day supply, percentiles) are data-driven since no predefined business benchmarks exist.
